# "1000 Agents" — the full film, all seven styles in one piece

You are directing and building the finished film: a ~40 s JavaScript animation of 1000 agents building an operating system from an empty folder, starring the coral block hero of the artist's "Fable Saga". The artist saw the same opening 3 seconds in seven art styles, liked them all, and asked for them to be COMBINED into one film "without making it look weird". This brief is the plan for doing that; follow it, and use your own judgement wherever a detail would look better done differently.

## The one idea that makes seven styles feel like one film
The styles are a ladder of fidelity that mirrors how software gets made: sketch → pixels → layered parts → blueprint → solid build → polished product. Each style is the world at one stage of the build, and the world "upgrades" each time the swarm hits a milestone. So a style change is never a random cut: it is an event in the story, caused by the agents, and the audience should be able to feel "they just levelled the world up".

Rules that keep it from looking weird (these matter more than anything else here):
1. THE HERO IS THE ANCHOR. Across every transition the hero is at the same screen position, the same apparent size, facing the same way, in the same pose phase (match-cut on the hero). Its coral never changes: #d97757 body, #a8553b shade, #f2a585 light, #6e2a1a dark. Two black eyes with a glint, four leg stubs, two arm nubs, mouth only when it calls out.
2. ONE PALETTE EVERYWHERE: deep indigo/near-black backgrounds (rich, never washed-out grey), coral family for everything alive (hero, agents, warm light), lilac/violet for everything built (folder, code, UI), a little teal only for "done/ok" marks. Normalise each style's background and exposure so cutting between them does not jump in brightness or hue (measure mean luminance and colour either side of each transition and match them).
3. ONE STAGING AXIS: hero left of centre, the thing being built right of centre, on a ground line at the same height in every style. Cameras may move within a section, but arrive back on this framing for each transition.
4. THE SWARM CARRIES EVERY TRANSITION: a wave of agents sweeps across the frame (or bursts outward from the build) and the new style is revealed behind/under the agents as they pass: a soft-edged wipe whose leading edge is made of agents, about 0.6–0.9 s, with both styles rendered underneath and composited through the mask. Vary the wave's direction and shape per transition so it never repeats (left-to-right sweep, radial bloom from the kernel, rising curtain, etc.). No hard cuts, no cross-dissolve-only transitions, no flashes.
5. ONE OVERLAY: a single HUD drawn identically on top of every style by the compositor (never by the style renderers; remove their own counters and "0 items" duplicates where they'd double up): top-left "AGENTS 0000" counting to 1000, and beneath it small counters that tell the story: FILES, LINES, then a stage label that changes with the build (SKETCH → CODE → MODULES → KERNEL → BOOT → DESKTOP). Small, lilac with coral numerals, one pixel-ish monospace drawn by you (no system font), with a faint dark backing so it reads on every style.
6. Agents are always small warm coral lights that move with purpose (streams, queues, hand-offs), whatever they are drawn as in each style.

- Work in `/home/user/brain/thousand-agents/film/`. Copy each style's code into `film/styles/<slot>/` and extend the copy with its section of the film (do not edit the originals in `thousand-agents/styles/`). Give every section a common interface: `renderAt(T)` in FILM time for its whole active range including the transition overlaps, with shared constants (timeline, palette, hero anchor position/size/pose function, seeded agent ids) in one shared file that every section loads, so the hero's pose and the agents' ids/positions are continuous across styles.
- A compositor (`film/compose.js` + a small page, or node with canvas via Playwright) that, for each frame: gets the frame from the active section (and the incoming section during a transition), builds the agent-wave mask for the transition, composites, then draws the shared HUD on top. Pipe frames into ffmpeg; never keep frame dumps (cache per-section intermediate videos losslessly or at very high quality if that helps, and delete them by name at the end). Deterministic: every frame a pure function of T; seeded PRNG; no Math.random/Date.
- A live page `film/index.html` that plays the whole film in a browser from file:// as well as it reasonably can (e.g. sections in iframes or modules switched on the timeline, with simplified transitions and reduced quality for the heavy 3D sections). No external assets or libraries anywhere. The video is the primary deliverable; the live page is best-effort and must not error.
- No sound.

## Quality bar and process
This is the artist's showcase post; it has to look deliberate and beautiful, not like a compilation. Build it roughly end to end first (every section blocked, every transition working), then polish in rounds. Each round: render a contact sheet (2 frames per second), a strip of consecutive frames across EVERY transition (every 2nd frame from 0.5 s before to 0.5 s after), and stills of each section's best moment; LOOK at them with the Read tool including 100% crops; critique as a ruthless motion designer and art director. Score 1–10: story clarity without captions; cohesion (does it feel like one film? do transitions feel caused and smooth? brightness/colour continuity; hero continuity); each section as a strong example of its style; the hero's fidelity and charm; swarm motion quality (purposeful, exactly 1000 agents once all are out); composition and restraint; darks and colour; pacing and the first-second hook; the ending. Fix everything below 9 and repeat; expect at least 5 rounds; don't report known weak sections, fix them. Specific things to watch: a transition where the hero jumps position or size; a section brighter or greyer than its neighbours; HUD doubled or jittering; the paper-cut or voxel sets getting busy; the wireframe hero being unrecognisable; clay beads blowing out to white; boot text looking like gibberish instead of elegant abstract marks; anything flashing (no more than 3 large-area luminance flashes per second; run a flash check over the whole film).
